# Geolocation from Images and Video

Systematic identification of locations from visual content. This is one of the most powerful OSINT techniques — a single image can reveal a precise location when analyzed methodically.

---

## Systematic Approach

Work from broad to narrow. Each step eliminates possibilities and focuses the search area.

### Step 1: Language and Script on Signs

The fastest way to narrow to a region or country.

| Script | Likely Region |
|--------|--------------|
| Latin alphabet | Americas, Europe, parts of Africa/Asia |
| Cyrillic | Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Serbia, Bulgaria, Mongolia, Central Asia |
| Arabic | Middle East, North Africa, parts of Southeast Asia |
| Devanagari | India (Hindi, Marathi, Nepali), Nepal |
| Thai script | Thailand |
| Hangul | South Korea, North Korea |
| Chinese characters (simplified) | Mainland China, Singapore |
| Chinese characters (traditional) | Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau |
| Japanese (mixed kanji/kana) | Japan |
| Ge'ez/Ethiopic | Ethiopia, Eritrea |
| Georgian | Georgia |
| Armenian | Armenia |
| Tamil | Tamil Nadu (India), Sri Lanka, Singapore |
| Khmer | Cambodia |
| Lao | Laos |
| Burmese | Myanmar |
| Sinhala | Sri Lanka |
| Tibetan | Tibet/China, Bhutan |

**Tips:**
- Look for bilingual signs — they narrow location significantly (e.g., French + Arabic = North Africa, English + Malay = Malaysia/Brunei)
- Store names, brand logos, and phone number formats are all clues
- Even a partial phone number gives you a country code or area code

### Step 2: Road Infrastructure

Roads are extraordinarily distinctive by country and region.

**Center Line Colors:**
- **Yellow center line, white edge lines** — United States, Canada
- **White center lines throughout** — Most of Europe, UK, Australia, Japan
- **Yellow center lines** — Also used in China, South Korea, parts of Latin America
- **No center lines** — Common in rural areas of developing countries

**Road Surface:**
- Asphalt (dark gray/black) — most developed countries
- Concrete (light gray, jointed) — common in Germany, parts of US interstates
- Red/laterite dirt — Sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South America, Australia outback
- Brick/cobblestone — Historic European city centers
- Gravel/white crushed rock — Rural Scandinavia, New Zealand

**Bollards and Posts:**
- **Red-and-white striped** — Netherlands, Belgium
- **Black and white** — UK (with reflectors)
- **Yellow** — United States
- **Green reflector posts** — France
- **Orange/red delineator posts** — Australia
- **Blue-and-white** — Greece
- **Chevron boards** — Style and color vary significantly by country

**Road Signs:**
- **Green highway signs** — US, Brazil, Saudi Arabia
- **Blue highway signs** — Most of Europe, Japan, South Korea, China
- **Brown signs** — Tourist/cultural attractions (nearly universal)
- **Diamond-shaped warning signs** — US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, parts of Latin America
- **Triangular warning signs** — Europe, most of Asia, Africa
- **Octagonal stop signs** — Nearly universal, but language varies (STOP vs PARE vs ARRÊT vs ALTO)

**Speed Limit Signs:**
- Red circle with number — Europe (Vienna Convention)
- Black and white rectangular — US, Canada
- Red circle, number inside — Most of the world
- Units: mph (US, UK, Myanmar) vs km/h (everywhere else)

### Step 3: Vegetation and Climate

**Tropical:**
- Dense rainforest canopy
- Palm trees — but which type matters:
  - Coconut palms — coastal tropics worldwide
  - Oil palms (neat plantation rows) — Malaysia, Indonesia, West Africa
  - Date palms — Middle East, North Africa
  - Royal palms — Caribbean, Central America
  - Fan palms (Borassus) — South/Southeast Asia, Africa
- Banana plants, bamboo groves
- Red/laterite soil visible

**Mediterranean:**
- Olive trees, cypress trees, umbrella pines
- Dry brown grass in summer, green in winter
- Vineyards on hillsides
- Scrubby maquis/chaparral vegetation

**Temperate Deciduous:**
- Broadleaf trees (oak, maple, beech, birch)
- Seasonal color changes
- Green rolling hills
- Mixed farming landscapes

**Boreal/Taiga:**
- Coniferous forests (spruce, pine, fir)
- Birch trees mixed in
- Snow cover for extended periods
- Flat to gently rolling terrain

**Arid/Desert:**
- Saguaro cactus — ONLY in Sonoran Desert (Arizona, NW Mexico)
- Joshua trees — Mojave Desert (California, Nevada)
- Spinifex grass — Australian outback
- Acacia trees — Africa, Middle East, Australia
- Sparse vegetation, rocky terrain
- Sand dunes (erg) vs rocky desert (reg/hamada)

**Specific Indicator Plants:**
- Eucalyptus (native) — Australia
- Baobab — Sub-Saharan Africa, Madagascar
- Araucaria (monkey puzzle) — Chile, southern Brazil
- Jacaranda (purple flowers) — widespread but origin South America/Southern Africa
- Cherry blossoms — Japan, South Korea (seasonal)
- Agave/century plant — Mexico, Mediterranean (introduced)

### Step 4: Architecture

**Roof Styles:**
- Flat roofs — Middle East, North Africa, arid regions, modern buildings worldwide
- Red/orange clay tile — Mediterranean, Latin America, parts of Southeast Asia
- Gray/blue slate — Northern France, UK, parts of US
- Green copper (patina) — Scandinavian churches, government buildings
- Steep pitched roofs — Northern Europe (snow shedding)
- Thatched — Rural UK, sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Southeast Asia
- Onion domes — Russia, Central Asia, Orthodox churches
- Pagoda-style tiered — East Asia, Southeast Asia
- Corrugated metal — Developing countries, rural areas worldwide

**Building Materials:**
- Red brick — UK, Netherlands, Northern Europe, Eastern US
- Yellow/brown brick — Central/Northern Germany, Denmark
- White stucco/plaster — Mediterranean, Latin America
- Timber frame (exposed) — Germany (Fachwerk), France (colombage), UK (Tudor)
- Adobe/mud brick — Middle East, North Africa, Southwestern US, West Africa
- Concrete block — Common in developing countries worldwide
- Wooden houses — Scandinavia, Russia, rural US, Japan

**Religious Architecture:**
- **Mosques:** Minarets (round/pencil = Ottoman/Turkey; square = North Africa/Moorish; spiral = Iraq), dome styles, geometric patterns
- **Orthodox churches:** Onion domes, iconostasis, often brightly colored (Russia) or white-washed (Greece)
- **Catholic churches:** Gothic spires, rose windows, bell towers
- **Protestant churches:** Generally simpler, steeples
- **Hindu temples:** Gopuram (tower gateway) — South India; Shikhara (curvilinear tower) — North India
- **Buddhist temples:** Stupas, pagodas, prayer flags
- **Shinto shrines:** Torii gates (red/orange), Japan only

**Housing Patterns:**
- Row houses/terraces — UK, Netherlands, Eastern US cities
- Courtyard houses — Middle East, North Africa, China (siheyuan)
- Compounds — West Africa, parts of Middle East
- High-rise apartments — East Asia, Soviet-era blocks (distinctive panel construction)
- Favelas/informal settlements — Latin America (brick, no finish), Africa (metal sheets)
- Suburban sprawl with lawns — US, Australia, Canada

### Step 5: Specific Identifiers

These pinpoint exact locations:

- **Street names** on signs — search in Google Maps
- **Business names** — search online, may have only one location
- **Highway route numbers** — look up the specific route
- **Kilometer markers** — used in many countries, give exact position
- **Cell tower IDs** — if visible on equipment
- **Bus route numbers** — searchable in transit databases
- **Postal codes** on buildings or mail
- **Emergency call numbers** on signs (911 = US/Canada, 112 = EU, 999 = UK)
- **Landmarks** — distinctive buildings, monuments, mountains, bridges

## Driving Side

**Drives on the LEFT (75 countries):**
UK, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Hong Kong, Macau, South Africa, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Jamaica, Trinidad & Tobago, Bahamas, Barbados, Cyprus, Malta, Suriname, Guyana, East Timor

**Drives on the RIGHT (165+ countries):**
US, Canada, Mexico, all of mainland Europe, Russia, China, South Korea, Brazil, Argentina, most of Africa, Middle East (except a few), Central Asia

**How to determine from an image:**
- Which side are cars parked on?
- Which side of the road is the driving lane?
- Dashboard camera position (driver side)
- Pedestrian crossing direction indicators
- Bus door side (usually curbside)

## License Plate Formats

**European Union:** White rectangular, blue strip on left with country code and EU stars
- Country letter codes: D (Germany), F (France), I (Italy), E (Spain), NL (Netherlands), etc.
- UK: Yellow rear plates, white front plates (no longer EU strip post-Brexit)

**United States:** Varies by state, generally rectangular with state name and design
- Format varies but typically 3 letters + 4 numbers or similar

**Russia:** White plate, black text, region code on right with Russian flag

**Japan:** White plates (private), green plates (commercial), yellow plates (kei cars)

**China:** Blue plates (standard), green plates (electric vehicles), region prefix character

**Brazil:** Gray plates with Mercosur format (4 letters + 3 numbers with QR code)

**Middle East:** Often Arabic and English numerals, distinctive colors by country

**India:** White (private) or yellow (commercial), state code + district + number

## Sun Position Analysis

Shadows reveal hemisphere, approximate latitude, and time of day.

**Basic Principles:**
- In the **Northern Hemisphere**, the sun is generally in the southern sky, so shadows point roughly NORTH
- In the **Southern Hemisphere**, the sun is generally in the northern sky, so shadows point roughly SOUTH
- Near the **equator**, shadows can point either direction depending on season
- **Short shadows** = midday or tropical latitudes
- **Long shadows** = early morning/late afternoon, or high latitudes

**Using Shadows for Time:**
1. Determine shadow direction (use compass rose on map if available)
2. Estimate shadow length relative to object height
3. Combine with approximate date
4. Reference `sun_position.py` for precise solar angle calculations

**Sun Position Script:**
```
python sun_position.py --lat 48.8566 --lon 2.3522 --date 2024-06-15 --time 14:30
```
Returns: solar azimuth, elevation, and shadow direction for given coordinates and time.

**Practical Application:**
- If you know the date and location but not the time: shadow angle reveals time
- If you know the date and time but not the location: shadow direction reveals latitude range
- Satellite imagery timestamps + shadow lengths = building height estimation

## Google Street View Metadata

**Camera Generations:**
- **Gen 1 (2007-2008):** Low resolution, visible camera rig on roof, blurry, limited coverage
- **Gen 2 (2008-2012):** Better resolution, smoother stitching, smaller camera rig
- **Gen 3 (2012-2017):** Higher resolution, better color, rosette pattern visible in sky
- **Gen 4 (2017+):** HD resolution, better stitching, smaller camera array

**Identifying the Capture Vehicle:**
- Google car — standard road coverage, shadow often visible
- Trekker (backpack) — hiking trails, narrow streets, indoor spaces
- Snowmobile — ski resorts, Arctic areas
- Trolley — museums, stadiums
- Third-party (Mapillary, KartaView) — often dashcam quality, different metadata

**Coverage Patterns:**
- Most roads covered: US, Canada, Western Europe, Japan, Australia, Brazil
- Partial coverage: Russia, India, China (no Google coverage — use Baidu/Tencent), Middle East
- Minimal coverage: Most of Africa, Central Asia, North Korea (zero)
- Some countries banned or restricted Street View: Germany (limited), Belarus, most of China

**Country-Specific Blurring:**
- Germany: Extensive building blurring (residents could opt out), limited new coverage
- Japan: Aggressive face and plate blurring, lower camera height
- France: Standard blurring
- Some countries blur government/military buildings

**URL Structure:**
Google Maps Street View URLs contain coordinates and heading:
```
https://www.google.com/maps/@48.8584,2.2945,3a,75y,90t/data=...
```
- `48.8584,2.2945` = latitude, longitude
- `3a` = zoom level
- `75y` = field of view
- `90t` = tilt

## Digital Clues in Images

**EXIF Data (reference `extract_exif.py`):**
- GPS coordinates — direct geolocation (if present)
- Camera make/model — narrows demographics (iPhone models popular in specific regions)
- Date/time — when taken (check if timezone-aware)
- Software field — editing software used
- Orientation — how the camera was held
- Focal length — lens type, affects perspective analysis
- Thumbnail — sometimes contains pre-edited version

**Platform-Specific Metadata:**
- **Twitter/X:** Strips all EXIF, resizes images
- **Facebook:** Strips EXIF but may store location separately if user enabled it
- **Instagram:** Strips EXIF, resizes, but location tags may be present in post
- **Flickr:** Often preserves EXIF including GPS (check image properties)
- **Google Photos shared links:** Strips EXIF for shared images
- **WhatsApp:** Strips EXIF, compresses heavily
- **Telegram:** Strips EXIF if sent as photo; preserves if sent as file/document
- **Signal:** Strips EXIF
- **Discord:** Strips EXIF

**Image Filename Clues:**
- `IMG_XXXX.jpg` — iPhone default naming
- `DSC_XXXX.jpg` — Nikon cameras
- `DSCF_XXXX.jpg` — Fujifilm cameras
- `Screenshot_YYYY-MM-DD` — Phone screenshots include date
- `MVIMG_` or `PANO_` — Google Pixel motion photos or panoramas
- Sequential numbering gaps may indicate deleted photos
